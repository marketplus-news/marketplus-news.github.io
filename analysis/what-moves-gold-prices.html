    What Moves the Gold Price? A Plain-English Guide to XAU/USD

    Real yields, the dollar, safe-haven demand and central bank buying: the forces behind XAU/USD.

    Published November 1, 2026 · 3 min read

    Educational content only. This explainer describes the general forces behind gold prices. It contains no forecasts or trading signals and is not financial advice.

    Gold is one of the most watched assets in the world, yet it has no earnings, pays no interest and has no central bank of its own. Its price is set by what buyers and sellers are willing to pay at any moment, and that willingness is shaped by a handful of recurring forces. Knowing them makes headlines about gold much easier to read.

    What XAU/USD means

    XAU is the international code for one troy ounce of gold, and XAU/USD is the price of that ounce in US dollars. Spot gold trades around the clock on weekdays in a global over-the-counter market centred on London, while gold futures trade on exchanges such as COMEX in New York. Spot and futures prices move together but are not identical: futures include the cost of carrying gold until the contract expires. The quotes strip on Market Plus shows COMEX gold futures for this reason.

    1. Interest rates and real yields

    Because gold pays no interest, holding it has an opportunity cost: money in gold is money not earning a bond yield. When real interest rates (yields after expected inflation) rise, that cost goes up and gold often comes under pressure. When real yields fall, or investors expect central banks to cut rates, gold tends to look more attractive. This is why decisions and comments from the US Federal Reserve so often move the gold price within minutes.

    2. The US dollar

    Gold is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes it more expensive for buyers using other currencies, and a weaker dollar makes it cheaper. As a result, gold and the dollar index frequently move in opposite directions. The relationship is a tendency, not a rule: in periods of stress both can rise together as investors look for safety.

    3. Inflation and confidence in money

    Gold has a long history as a store of value, and many investors buy it when they worry that inflation will erode the purchasing power of paper currencies. In practice the link with month-to-month inflation is loose; expectations about future inflation and about how central banks will respond matter more than any single data release.

    4. Safe-haven demand

    Wars, financial crises, banking scares and sharp stock market falls tend to push some investors into assets they see as safer. Gold is a classic choice because it carries no credit risk: it cannot default. Geopolitical headlines can therefore lift gold quickly, although such moves sometimes fade once the initial shock passes.

    5. Central banks and investment flows

    Central banks hold gold as part of their reserves, and their buying or selling is a significant, slow-moving source of demand. Investors also buy gold through bars and coins, and through exchange-traded funds (ETFs) backed by physical metal; inflows and outflows from these funds are published and closely watched.

    6. Physical demand and supply

    Jewellery, technology and industrial uses make up another part of demand, with strong seasonal patterns around festivals and weddings in large markets such as India and China. On the supply side, mine production changes slowly because new mines take many years to develop, and recycled gold adds supply when prices are high.

    How to read gold headlines

    • Rates news (central bank decisions, inflation and jobs reports) usually matters most in the short term.
    • Dollar moves often explain a day's change in gold without any gold-specific news.
    • Geopolitical shocks can cause sharp but sometimes temporary moves.
    • Flows from central banks and ETFs shape the longer trend.

    No single factor explains every move, and the forces often pull in different directions at the same time. Treat any explanation of "why gold moved today" as a reasonable interpretation, not a certainty.
