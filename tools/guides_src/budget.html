A budget is simply a plan for your money before the month starts, instead of a surprise when it ends. You do not need special software or a finance degree. This guide walks you through building your first monthly budget in a few steps, then explains two popular beginner methods, the 50/30/20 rule and zero-based budgeting, with worked examples you can copy.

Step 1: Work out your monthly take-home income

Use the money that actually arrives in your account after tax and deductions, not your gross salary. If you are paid monthly, look at your net pay. If you are paid weekly or every two weeks, multiply a typical paycheck by the number of paychecks in a year and divide by 12, so you get a fair monthly figure. If your income varies, as with freelance or shift work, use your lowest typical month as the baseline and treat anything above it as a bonus.

Step 2: List your fixed bills

Write down everything that costs roughly the same every month: rent or mortgage, utilities, internet, phone, insurance, loan minimum payments, subscriptions and transport passes. Check bank statements so nothing is missed. These are the commitments your budget has to cover first.

Step 3: Track one month of spending

For one month, record every purchase, or download your bank and card statements and sort the transactions into categories such as groceries, eating out, transport, shopping, fun and household. You are not judging yourself yet, just collecting facts. Most people are surprised by one or two categories, and those are exactly where a budget helps.

Method 1: The 50/30/20 rule

The 50/30/20 rule splits your take-home pay into three buckets:

  • 50% for needs: housing, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, minimum debt payments.
  • 30% for wants: eating out, entertainment, hobbies, travel, non-essential shopping.
  • 20% for savings and extra debt payments: emergency fund, retirement, paying debt faster.

Example with a take-home income of $3,000 a month:

BucketShareAmount
Needs50%$1,500
Wants30%$900
Savings and debt20%$600
Total100%$3,000

The strength of this method is simplicity: three numbers to watch. Its weakness is that your needs may not fit into 50%, especially if housing is expensive where you live. That is fine. Treat the percentages as a starting point, then adjust: for example 60/20/20 while rent is high, and protect the savings share as much as you can.

Method 2: Zero-based budgeting

In a zero-based budget, you give every dollar of income a job until income minus planned spending and saving equals zero. "Zero" does not mean you spend everything; savings are one of the jobs. It takes more effort, but you see exactly where each dollar is going.

Example with the same $3,000:

Income $3,000 − plan $3,000 = $0, so every dollar has a job. Notice that in this example the needs add up to $2,190, about 73% of income, so it would not fit a strict 50/30/20 split. A zero-based budget makes that trade-off visible and lets you decide consciously where to cut.

Which method should you choose?

  • Choose 50/30/20 if you want something quick, your income is steady and you mainly need guardrails.
  • Choose zero-based if money feels tight, your income varies, you are paying off debt or you want to know exactly where everything goes.
  • Combine them: use 50/30/20 as the big-picture target and a zero-based plan for the details.

Plan for irregular expenses with sinking funds

Many budgets break because of costs that do not arrive every month: annual insurance, car repairs, birthdays, holidays, school costs. A sinking fund spreads them out. Take the yearly cost, divide by 12 and save that amount monthly. A $1,200 annual car insurance bill becomes $100 a month; $600 of holiday gifts becomes $50 a month. When the bill comes, the money is already there.

Build a weekly 15-minute check-in

A budget written once and never checked will drift. Pick a fixed time each week and:

  1. Record or import the week's transactions.
  2. Compare each category with its budget.
  3. Look at what is left for the rest of the month.
  4. Decide on any adjustments now, not at the end of the month.

What to do when you overspend

Overspending in a category is normal, especially in the first few months. Do not abandon the budget. Instead:

  • Move money on purpose from another category, such as wants, to cover it. The total stays the same.
  • Find the cause. Was it a one-off, or is the category simply too small? If it happens every month, the budget is unrealistic, so raise that line and lower another.
  • Avoid covering gaps with credit where you can; if you do, add the repayment to next month's plan.
  • Expect it to take a few months. Each month, your numbers get closer to real life.

FAQ

Is 50/30/20 based on gross or net income?

It is usually applied to take-home (net) pay, after tax and deductions, because that is the money you can actually spend and save.

Do I have to track every coffee?

Not forever. Tracking everything for the first month or two shows you where money goes; after that, many people track by category or check bank statements weekly.

What should I save for first?

That depends on your situation. A common starting point is a small emergency fund, so an unexpected bill does not go on a credit card. If you have high-interest debt or complex questions, consider talking to a qualified adviser.
