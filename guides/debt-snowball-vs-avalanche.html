    Debt Snowball vs Avalanche, Explained

    Two ways to order the same payments. One usually saves more interest; the other gives quicker wins. Here is how to choose.

    Published November 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    Try it with your numbers: Debt Snowball vs Avalanche Calculator (free)→

    If you have several debts, such as credit cards, a car loan and a personal loan, you need a plan for which one to attack first. The two best-known plans are the debt snowball and the debt avalanche. They share the same engine and differ only in the order.

    The shared engine

    1. List every debt with its balance, interest rate (APR) and minimum payment.
    2. Decide on a fixed total monthly amount: all the minimums plus an extra amount you can keep paying.
    3. Pay the minimum on every debt, and put all the extra on one target debt.
    4. When the target is paid off, add its former payment to the next target. The payment "rolls" and grows.

    Debt snowball: smallest balance first

    The snowball targets the debt with the smallest balance, regardless of interest rate. The first debt disappears quickly, which frees up its minimum payment and gives a visible win. For many people that early progress is what keeps them going for the months or years a payoff takes.

    Debt avalanche: highest interest rate first

    The avalanche targets the debt with the highest interest rate. Because the extra money always goes where interest is most expensive, it minimises total interest and generally finishes no later than the snowball. The downside: if the highest-rate debt also has a large balance, the first win can take a long time.

    A worked comparison

    Take four example debts: a $1,200 card at 19.9% APR (minimum $40), a $3,200 card at 24.9% ($95), a $4,000 personal loan at 12% ($130) and an $8,500 car loan at 7.5% ($260), with $200 a month on top of the minimums. Running them through our snowball vs avalanche calculator (monthly interest, payments rolled forward) gives:

    • Snowball clears the $1,200 card in month 6, the $3,200 card in month 16, the personal loan in month 21 and the car loan in month 27. Total interest: about $2,277.
    • Avalanche clears the $3,200 card first, in month 13, then the $1,200 card in month 16, and finishes on the same schedule. Total interest: about $2,217.
    • Minimums only, without rolling payments forward, takes almost five years and costs about $4,669 in interest.

    In this example the avalanche saves about $60 and both finish in the same month, while the snowball delivers its first paid-off debt seven months earlier. The real lesson is the third line: either method roughly halves the interest compared with paying minimums. With other numbers, especially a large balance at a high rate, the gap between the two methods can be much bigger, so test your own debts.

    How to choose

    • Small interest difference? Pick the method you will stick with. For many people that is the snowball.
    • Large difference, usually a big high-rate balance? The avalanche saves real money.
    • Hybrid: clear one or two tiny balances for momentum, then switch to avalanche order.

    Make it stick

    Automate the minimum payments so nothing is missed, schedule the extra payment for payday, and track progress monthly. Avoid adding new debt to the cards you are paying off. If a minimum payment does not cover the monthly interest, that debt will never shrink on its own; contact the lender or a non-profit credit counsellor about options.
