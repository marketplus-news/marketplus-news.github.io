    How to Keep a Trading Journal: What to Log and Why

    Updated October 25, 2026 · 5 min read

    Educational content only. This guide explains how to record and review your own trades. It contains no trading signals or strategy recommendations and is not financial advice. Trading involves a substantial risk of loss.

    Memory is a poor record of trading. Winning trades feel like skill, losing trades feel like bad luck, and after a few weeks it is hard to say what really happened. A trading journal fixes that by writing down every trade with the same set of facts, so you can review your own process with real data instead of impressions. A journal cannot make a strategy profitable or remove risk, but it can show you whether you followed your own rules, where your mistakes repeat and how much you are actually risking.

    Why keep a trading journal?

    • To check discipline. Did you trade the plan you wrote, or something else?
    • To measure risk honestly. Seeing risk per trade in money and as a share of your account makes oversized positions obvious.
    • To find repeating mistakes, such as moving stops, chasing entries or trading out of boredom.
    • To separate process from outcome. A trade can follow your rules and still lose, or break them and still win. Over time, the journal shows which habits help and which hurt.

    What to log for every trade

    Use the same fields every time, and fill in the plan before you enter, while your reasoning is not yet influenced by the result.

    Before the trade

    • Date and time (with time zone) and instrument, e.g. a stock, currency pair or index.
    • Direction: long or short.
    • Setup and reason: in one or two sentences, why this trade fits your written plan.
    • Planned entry, stop-loss and target.
    • Position size: shares, contracts or lots.
    • Risk amount: how much you lose if the stop is hit (distance to stop × position size), and risk as a % of your account.

    After the trade

    • Actual entry and exit prices, and the reason for the exit (stop, target, manual).
    • Result in money, both before and after fees, commissions and other costs.
    • R-multiple (explained below).
    • Screenshots of the chart at entry and at exit.
    • Emotions: calm, rushed, fearful, overconfident, bored. One word is enough.
    • Rules followed? A simple yes or no.
    • Mistakes and lessons: what you would do differently, if anything.

    How to calculate the R-multiple

    The R-multiple expresses a trade's result in units of the risk you planned to take. "1R" is the amount you would have lost if the stop had been hit.

    R-multiple = result of the trade ÷ planned risk

    Example: you buy 100 shares at $50 with a stop-loss at $48. Your planned risk is 100 × ($50 − $48) = $200, so 1R = $200. On a $10,000 account, that is 2% of the account.

    • If you exit at $53, the result is 100 × $3 = $300, which is +1.5R.
    • If the stop is hit at $48, the result is −$200, which is −1R.
    • If you exit at $49, the result is −$100, which is −0.5R.

    R makes trades of different sizes comparable and immediately exposes a problem many journals reveal: losses bigger than −1R, which usually mean the stop was moved or ignored. Remember to include fees; in this example, $2 of commissions would turn a $300 gain into $298.

    Review metrics, weekly and monthly

    Logging is half the work. The other half is a regular review. Once a week, read through your trades and notes. Once a month, calculate a few numbers. Small samples are noisy, so read them as a description of the past, not a forecast.

    • Number of trades, and how many followed your rules.
    • Win rate = winning trades ÷ total trades.
    • Average win and average loss, in money and in R.
    • Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss). It shows the average result per trade over the period.
    • Maximum drawdown: the largest fall from a peak in your account balance to a later low.
    • Total fees and costs.

    An example of why these numbers belong together: suppose 20 trades had a 40% win rate, an average win of +1.5R and an average loss of −1R. Expectancy = 0.4 × 1.5 − 0.6 × 1 = 0R. Before costs, that period broke even, and after fees it lost money, even though some individual wins looked impressive. For drawdown: if an account rose to $10,500 and later fell to $9,800 before making a new high, the maximum drawdown was $700, or about 6.7% of the peak.

    Also sort trades by setup, instrument, day of week and the "rules followed" column. Comparing trades that followed the plan with those that did not is often the most useful part of the review.

    Common journaling mistakes

    1. Logging only some trades. Skipping the embarrassing ones makes every statistic misleading.
    2. Writing the reason after the result. Record the plan before entry so hindsight cannot rewrite it.
    3. Ignoring costs. Fees, spreads and commissions belong in the result.
    4. Collecting data but never reviewing it. Schedule the weekly and monthly reviews in advance.
    5. Drawing big conclusions from a few trades. A handful of results can be pure chance.

    FAQ

    Should I journal demo or paper trades?

    Yes. Journaling practice trades builds the habit and the rule-checking routine before real money is involved.

    Spreadsheet or notebook?

    Either works. A spreadsheet can calculate R, win rate and expectancy for you, which makes monthly reviews faster; a notebook is fine if you review it consistently.

    Will a trading journal make me profitable?

    No tool can promise that. A journal is a record that helps you understand your own decisions and risk. Results depend on many factors, and losses are always possible.
